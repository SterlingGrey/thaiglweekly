The Ledger and the Rose

Cold Open · The Arraignment

The air conditioning in Division Four of the Bangkok South Civil Court did not merely cool the room; it embalmed it. Outside the tall casement windows, the late October heat shimmered off the asphalt of Charoen Krung Road in greasy waves, but inside, the temperature hovered at a brisk, institutional sixty-four degrees. The room smelled of lemon wood-oil, damp wool from a passing afternoon squall, and the faint, sour tang of old paper.

High on the paneled teak wall behind the empty bench, the gilded wings of the royal Garuda caught the pale fluorescent light.

Anin sat at the defendant’s table with her spine an inch clear of the mahogany chair back, her hands folded loosely over a single cream-colored legal pad. Over her bespoke charcoal silk suit, she wore the mandatory black khrui, the traditional Thai barrister’s robe. On nine out of ten lawyers in the building, the sheer black gauze sleeves and pleated shoulders looked like a limp graduation gown pulled from the bottom of a closet. On Anin, at twenty-eight, it draped like couture.

She was used to being stared at in courtrooms. Four years after passing the Thai bar at the top of her class, half the senior clerks in the building still found excuses to linger near her table whenever she walked in, drawn by the lethal combination of high, aristocratic cheekbones, dark, cat-sharp eyes, and the kind of cool, long-legged poise that belonged on a billboard above Siam Paragon rather than a commercial tribunal.

To her left, her client was ruining the aesthetic. Direk Voravong, the fifty-two-year-old Chief Executive Officer of Voravong-Kerry Pacific Logistics, was thick around the jowls and currently sweating through an imported Italian shirt despite the meat-locker chill. Every twenty seconds, his eyes darted sideways toward Anin’s profile before dropping nervously back to his twitching thumb.

“Stop doing that,” Anin murmured. She didn’t turn her head, and her glossed lips barely moved.

Direk froze. “My wife’s brothers are sitting in the back row, Khun Anin. When Somchai collapsed this morning, I thought the plaintiffs would drop the petition or beg for a three-month postponement. Why are we even sitting here at four o’clock in the afternoon?”

“Because when a lead counsel lands in Bumrungrad Hospital with a ruptured appendix twenty-four hours before witness examination begins, the Court requires a formal substitution of counsel on the record,” Anin said, her voice a low, unhurried contralto that carried no further than Direk’s right ear. “Breathe through your nose. You look like a man who hid sixty-four million baht in a mattress, rather than an executive whose accounting department made a clerical error.”

Direk swallowed hard and straightened his silk tie. Anin let her gaze drift lazily across the center aisle toward the plaintiff’s table.

When word had come down at noon that sixty-year-old Somchai Chavalit was under a surgeon’s knife, Anin had expected Chavalit’s firm to send one of their senior litigators to beg for a continuance.

Instead, they had sent... this.

The young woman sitting behind the plaintiff’s placard couldn’t have been more than twenty-three or twenty-four, barely six months past her bar certification, judging by the high, freshly minted registration number typed at the bottom of the emergency substitution sheet. When she had walked down the center aisle ten minutes earlier, Anin had noticed with a flicker of surprise that they were nearly identical in height and build: long-limbed, slender-shouldered, carrying the unmistakable upright posture of girls raised in the pleated skirts of Bangkok’s elite convent academies.

And, Anin had noted with the cool, clinical objectivity she applied to everything in a room, the girl was startlingly pretty. Even hunched over three overstuffed red-rope accordion binders, there was no hiding the delicate, heart-shaped symmetry of her face, the porcelain skin flushed pale rose at the hollow of her throat, or the dark lashes thick enough to touch the lenses of her crooked wire-rimmed glasses.

That, however, was where the girl’s advantages ended.

Her navy jacket was two sizes too loose in the shoulders beneath her black khrui robe, her dark hair was twisted up at the back of her head with a tortoiseshell claw clip that was losing a war of attrition against several silky strands, and she was staring down at her witness list with an expression of pure, unadulterated terror.

Lin, the substitution notice read. Phattara Lin.

Six months out of university, Anin thought, the corner of her mouth curving into a slow, private smirk. Chavalit didn’t send a replacement fighter. They sent a sacrificial lamb to hold the chair warm. God, this is going to be the easiest fee check of my year.

The side door behind the dais clicked open. A bailiff stepped out and called the room to order.

Everyone in the gallery rose and bowed their heads in silent respect as Presiding Judge Prakit and a younger quorum judge entered in their black judicial robes, settling into the two high-backed leather chairs beneath the Garuda emblem. There was no wooden gavel, only the quiet rustle of silk robes and the click of Judge Prakit switching on the gooseneck microphone mounted to the teak desk in front of him.

“Be seated,” Judge Prakit said, his voice amplified slightly through the room’s speakers, carrying the bone-deep fatigue of a jurist who had spent thirty years listening to wealthy families lie about arithmetic. He pulled the thick blue court file to the center of his blotter. “Black Case Number Phor 1104/2567. The Shareholder Recovery Trust of Voravong-Kerry Pacific Logistics, Plaintiff, versus Direk Voravong, Defendant. We are convened at late notice regarding an emergency petition for substitution of plaintiff’s counsel and final verification of the Banchi Phayan exhibit schedules prior to tomorrow morning’s witness examination. For the defense?”

Anin rose smoothly, the sheer black sleeves of her lawyer’s robe falling in clean, unbroken lines as she bowed her head toward the bench. “Anin Rattanakosin, Senior Associate for Sterling & Vachira, appearing as lead counsel for the defendant, Mr. Direk Voravong, Your Honor.”

Judge Prakit gave her a brief, knowing nod. Everyone in the commercial courts knew Anin. Four years of high-stakes corporate litigation, eleven major defenses, eleven clean dismissals. “Good afternoon, Khun Anin. And appearing on the emergency substitution for the plaintiff?”

Across the aisle, the rookie scrambled to her feet.

She moved a fraction too fast. As she stood, the wide, pleated black gauze sleeve of her khrui robe snagged hard on the brass corner-bracket of the heavy wooden table. It yanked her right arm backward mid-motion. Startled, Lin jerked her elbow free, accidentally sweeping her forearm directly through a secondary stack of loose duplicate schedules and her own stainless-steel water flask.

Clang.

The metal flask hit the hardwood floor like a dropped wrench, rolling noisily toward the center aisle. A white avalanche of A4 paper followed it, fanning out across the polished parquet in a four-foot radius.

In the gallery behind them, one of Direk’s brothers-in-law let out a poorly stifled snort. Beside Anin, Direk visibly deflated with relief.

Anin didn’t move. She remained standing, turning her head thirty degrees to watch as the younger lawyer dropped awkwardly to one knee, her black robe tangling around her calves as she frantically scooped at the scattered sheets. When Lin looked up, a flush of bright, mortified crimson had crept up from the open collar of her white blouse, staining her throat and her cheekbones. She caught her plush lower lip between her teeth, trying and failing to shove the pages back into numerical order.

Oh, sweetheart, Anin thought, a warm, predatory amusement curling low in her chest as her gaze lingered for a beat on the flushed skin of the rookie’s neck. Did nobody tell you to pin your sleeves before standing up in Division Four? I’m going to eat you alive before the noon recess.

“I... I apologize to the Court, Your Honor,” Lin stammered, rising back to her feet with a crooked sheaf of papers clutched tight against her ribs like a shield. A single white exhibit sheet was stuck to the heel of her sensible black pump. “Phattara Lin, Your Honor. Junior Associate with Chavalit & Associates, appearing on emergency substitution for... for the prosecution.”

Judge Prakit peered over the rims of his half-moon spectacles, his expression deadpan as the junior judge beside him hid a wince behind his hand. “This is a civil commercial tribunal, Ms. Lin. There is no prosecution. You represent the chot, the plaintiff.”

Lin blinked rapidly behind her crooked wire frames, her throat working as she swallowed. “Yes! Of course. Forgive me, Your Honor. The plaintiff. Under Section... under Section 420 of the Civil and Commercial Code, no, wait, Section 85 of the Public Limited Companies Act...”

“The Settlement of Issues was finalized four months ago, Counselor. We do not need you to recite the code today,” Judge Prakit cut in dryly. He leaned three inches closer to his gooseneck microphone, dictating directly to the court clerk typing at the desk below the bench: ”Record that Plaintiff’s lead counsel, Mr. Somchai Chavalit, is incapacitated due to medical emergency. Ms. Phattara Lin enters her appearance as substitute counsel. Both parties have confirmed the previously filed witness and document lists regarding the disputed disbursement of sixty-four million, eight hundred thousand baht across forty-two contested vendor invoices to Siam Apex Warehousing, Golden Delta Freight, and Nonthaburi Cloud Systems.”

Judge Prakit released the microphone button and looked down over the bench at the defense table. “Khun Anin, given the sudden change in plaintiff’s representation, does the defense have any preliminary motions regarding the witness schedule for tomorrow morning?”

Anin didn’t even glance down at her pad. Her voice filled the quiet room, velvet over cold steel.

“None, Your Honor. Our position remains unchanged from the Settlement of Issues. Every vendor cited in the plaintiff’s petition was vetted through standard procurement protocols, every invoice bears a registered corporate seal approved by the internal audit committee, and every disbursement was ratified by the board’s own quarterly sign-offs. We are fully prepared to cross-examine the plaintiff’s witnesses at nine o’clock tomorrow morning, and we anticipate moving for full dismissal as soon as the plaintiff rests.”

Out of the corner of her eye, Anin watched Lin across the aisle. The younger woman was furiously scribbling a note onto the back of a manila folder, pressing down so hard the tip of her pen tore a small jagged hole right through the cardstock. Lin winced, bit her lip again, and tucked a fallen lock of dark hair behind her ear with trembling fingers.

From this angle, with the late afternoon sunlight slanting through the tall windows to catch the curve of the rookie’s jaw and the dark, liquid shine of her eyes behind those ridiculous glasses, Anin felt an unexpected, entirely unprofessional tug of interest.

A genuine shame, Anin mused, smoothing the front of her black robe before sitting down. If she weren’t sitting at that table, she’d be a lot of fun to take apart over a bottle of wine after I ruin her week.

“Ms. Lin,” Judge Prakit said, folding his hands on the blotter. “Given that Mr. Somchai left you holding a sixty-four-million-baht commercial file this morning, the Court is willing to entertain a motion for continuance if you are unprepared to conduct witness examination alone tomorrow.”

Lin stood up again, keeping both hands pressed flat on top of her red-rope binders this time, as if terrified her own sleeves might betray her twice. She risked a fleeting, wide-eyed glance across the center aisle at Anin.

For two full seconds, their gazes locked. Behind those crooked wire frames, Lin’s dark eyes looked huge, vulnerable, and completely out of their depth.

She looked away first, ducking her chin toward the bench. “No continuance, Your Honor. The shareholders have waited fourteen months for a hearing date. I... I’ll be ready at nine o’clock.”

“See that your exhibit binders stay on the desk tomorrow, Counselor,” Judge Prakit said. He switched off his microphone and rose with his fellow judge. “The Court is adjourned until nine o’clock tomorrow morning for witness examination.”

Everyone stood and bowed their heads as the two judges stepped down from the dais and disappeared into chambers.

As the low murmur of conversation flooded back into the chilly courtroom, Anin unclipped her black khrui robe from her shoulders, folding the sheer fabric neatly over her arm without once looking back across the aisle to see if the rookie had found her water bottle.

“Go home, Direk,” Anin said, sliding her Montblanc pen into her leather portfolio. “Pour yourself a scotch and take your wife to dinner. By Thursday afternoon, that girl is going to wish she’d applied to medical school.”
