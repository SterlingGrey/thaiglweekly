Tide & Shadow

Chapter 1 · The Last Crossing

The Gulf of Thailand did not warn a man before it broke him; it simply shifted its weight, and the horizon vanished.

Dr. Tawan stood on the grated steel catwalk of Caisson Alpha, her boots locked against the salt-crusted deck plates, and watched the supply cutter Chonburi pull away from the mooring pylons. The vessel’s diesel exhaust hung heavy in the humid, dead air, a greasy smudge against a sky the color of a bruised plum.

On the lower landing stage, where the cutter had dropped her ten minutes earlier, a lone figure in a stiff, charcoal-gray corporate suit stood gripping the safety rail. Khun Khem. Auditor, Risk Management Division, Vorakit Holdings, Bangkok. Sent down to inspect an isolated marine engineering station that hadn't turned a profit in three fiscal quarters, and apparently determined to do it with all the warmth of a monsoon squall.

Tawan adjusted the collar of her worn canvas jacket, her tall, broad-shouldered frame braced effortlessly against the rising swell. She had spent six years designing sub-surface anchors in these deep-water trenches; she knew every rivet, every fatigue crack, and every murmur of the steel caisson beneath her feet. She also knew that a corporate auditor from Sathorn Road wouldn't know the difference between a cathodic protection anode and a structural fault line if it bit her on the ankle.

A sharp crackle from the handheld radio on her hip broke the rhythmic thud of the waves.

"Dr. Tawan," Khem’s voice came through the speaker, crisp, clipped, and irritatingly clear despite the static of the gathering gale. "The generator in sub-level two is vibrating three decibels above safety threshold parameters according to my transmission log. Is your maintenance team aware, or are we simply waiting for a catastrophic failure before logging it?"

Tawan pressed the transmit button, her deep voice a low, steady rumble against the wind.

"The generator is humming because it’s carrying double load while the primary desalination pump cycles, Khun Khem. If you’d checked the physical logbook instead of your tablet before stepping off the boat, you’d know that."

A short pause hissed over the radio channel.

"I prefer raw data to handwritten folklore, Doctor," Khem retorted smoothly. "I'll be in the central office. See that the telemetry archives for the last twelve months are decrypted and ready for my review by nineteen hundred hours."

The radio clicked dead.

Tawan stared out across the darkening expanse of water, where the jagged silhouette of Koh Phra Mae was rapidly being swallowed by a towering wall of slate-gray clouds. The barometer on the weather station wall dropped four points in thirty seconds.

It wasn't just a squall. Typhoon Nari had changed course, and Caisson Alpha was right in its path.

“We are entirely isolated until the typhoon clears.”
“Isolated. Just you, me, and three thousand gigabytes of financial records.”
