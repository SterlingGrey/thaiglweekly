02 / THE ESSAY

A Ticket Is Not a Claim on Someone

Opinion · By Stephanie Grey

I am tired of watching affection turn into a demand for control.

Someone gives a good performance. We like the series. We like the chemistry. We watch interviews, buy tickets, follow the next project. Somewhere along the way, some people decide that all this attention has earned them a say in how the women involved should live.

Then a private life becomes an offence. Someone must explain herself. Someone must apologise. People start looking for evidence of a betrayal that exists only because they have mistaken their own expectations for an agreement.

I think that is horribly unfair. And I am not interested in helping it along.

If a series disappoints you, say so. If a script wastes good actors, write about it. If a company takes your money and fails to deliver what it advertised, demand an answer. There is plenty in this business that deserves a hard look. I have no intention of turning this publication into a place where everything gets a polite round of applause.

But a person’s private relationships are not a production decision we get to overrule.

The women making these shows are doing a job. They learn lines, rehearse, work through scenes and try to make us believe in something. When it works, we feel it. That is one of the pleasures of watching a good romance. I want to be caught up in it. I want the hesitation before a kiss to matter.

The performance can be convincing without creating an obligation to continue it after the cameras stop.

I also understand that the industry can make that boundary harder to see. When a promotional campaign invites an audience to feel personally close to a pair, the companies behind it should take responsibility for how they sell that closeness. They should be able to promote a romance without making the performers’ privacy part of the bargain.

Fans can question that marketing. They can decide they have had enough of it. What they cannot reasonably do is make an individual woman pay for every expectation a campaign encouraged.

“But I supported her” is the sentence that bothers me most when it is used as a threat.

You bought the ticket. You attended the event. You paid for the merchandise. You are entitled to what was offered in that transaction. You did not purchase the right to approve her friends, her relationships or the parts of her life she chooses to keep to herself.

She does not owe you a different life because you spent money enjoying her work.

Disappointment is allowed. You can lose interest. You can stop buying tickets. Nobody has to remain a fan forever, and nobody needs permission to leave. There is a perfectly decent way to do that: leave without organising a punishment on the way out.

The excuse that this is simply the price of fame does very little for me. A recognisable face does not tell us how secure someone’s career is, what she earns or what she can bear. We see a finished episode and a few polished appearances. We do not see an entire working life. We certainly do not acquire ownership of it.

And a crowd does not become harmless because each person thinks they have only contributed one comment. From the receiving end, the comments arrive together. So do the demands, the accusations and the insistence that silence proves something.

I would like us to stop excusing that as enthusiasm.

Here at Thai GL Weekly, we have a choice about what we put in front of readers. We can discuss the work, follow the announced projects and publish criticism we are prepared to stand behind. We can enjoy the romance without turning uncertainty about somebody’s private life into another reason to click.

That is the choice I want us to make.

There are good stories here. There are performances worth our time, writers worth reading and women doing work we want to see more of. Give that work your attention. Argue about it. Love it enough to expect something from it.

Leave the people making it room to live.
